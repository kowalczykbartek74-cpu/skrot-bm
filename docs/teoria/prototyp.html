Inflation

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, so each złoty buys less than before.

1

What inflation is (and what it is not)

One price going up is not inflation. Inflation is about prices on average, over time.

  • DefinicjaGeneral price level

    Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level (the average price of all goods and services in the economy). "Sustained" means it lasts, not a one-off jump.

    • Nie mylInflation vs a relative price change

      If butter gets 30% dearer while phones get cheaper, that is a change in relative prices (one price against another). It is inflation only if the average of all prices rises.

  • DefinicjaPurchasing power

    Purchasing power is how much you can buy with a given amount of money. Inflation lowers it: with 4% inflation, 100 zł buys what about 96 zł bought a year earlier.

  • Nie mylDisinflation, deflation, hyperinflation

    TermWhat happens to pricesExample
    Disinflationstill rise, but more slowly6% → 3%
    Deflationfall on average−1%
    Hyperinflationrise extremely fast (a common rule of thumb: over 50% a month)Zimbabwe 2008

    • Typowy błąd"Inflation fell, so prices fell"

      A fall from 6% to 3% is disinflation: prices are still 3% higher than a year ago. Prices fall only when inflation is below zero.

2

How we measure it

We cannot track every price, so we follow a price index: one number that summarises many prices.

  • DefinicjaConsumer Price Index (CPI)

    The CPI measures the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services that a typical household buys (food, rent, fuel, transport, services), compared with a base year. In Poland it is published by Statistics Poland (GUS).

    • WzórIndex and inflation rate

      CPI = cost of basket this year / cost of basket in base year × 100 base year: CPI = 100
      inflation rate = (CPIthis year − CPIlast year) / CPIlast year × 100%

    • PrzykładA basket in Szczecin

      1. The basket cost 500 zł in the base year and 520 zł this year.
      2. CPI = 520 / 500 × 100 = 104.
      3. Inflation = (104 − 100) / 100 × 100% = 4%.

    • Typowy błądSubtracting index points

      If CPI goes from 120 to 126, inflation is not 6%. It is (126 − 120) / 120 × 100% = 5%. Divide by the old value.

  • DefinicjaGDP deflator

    The GDP deflator measures the price level of everything produced in the country, not only what households buy.

    GDP deflator = nominal GDP / real GDP × 100

    • Przykład

      Nominal GDP = 1,100 bn zł, real GDP = 1,000 bn zł. Deflator = 1,100 / 1,000 × 100 = 110: prices are 10% higher than in the base year.

    • Nie mylCPI vs GDP deflator

      CPIGDP deflator
      Coversgoods households buy, including importseverything produced at home, no imports
      Basketfixedchanges with what is produced
      Usecost of living, wages, pensionsturning nominal GDP into real GDP

      Example: dearer imported oil raises the CPI quickly, the deflator much less.

  • DefinicjaCore inflation

    Core inflation leaves out the most volatile prices, usually food and energy. It shows the underlying trend that the central bank can influence. The National Bank of Poland (NBP) publishes several core measures.

  • IdeaWhy the CPI tends to overstate the cost of living

    • Substitution bias: when beef gets dearer, people buy more chicken, but the fixed basket still counts beef.
    • Quality change: a new laptop costs more but is also better; part of the price rise is "more laptop".
    • New goods: new products enter the basket late, after their prices have already fallen.
3

Why prices rise

Four causes. On an exam, first decide whether the push comes from buyers (demand), from costs, or from money.

  • DefinicjaDemand-pull inflation

    Total spending (aggregate demand) grows faster than the economy can produce. Firms cannot make more quickly, so they raise prices. Typical triggers: tax cuts, large government spending, cheap credit, a boom in exports.

    • Połączenie

      Aggregate demand and the multiplier: Lekcja 8

  • DefinicjaCost-push inflation

    Production costs rise (energy, raw materials, wages not matched by productivity), and firms pass them on in prices. Output often falls at the same time, which is called stagflation (stagnation + inflation).

    • Nie mylWhich one is it?

      Prices up and output up → demand-pull. Prices up and output down → cost-push.

  • IdeaToo much money: the quantity theory

    In the long run, inflation is driven by money growing faster than real output.

    M × V = P × Y M money supply · V velocity (how often a złoty is spent per year) · P price level · Y real output
    in growth rates: %ΔM + %ΔV ≈ %ΔP + %ΔY

    • Przykład

      Money grows by 8%, velocity is stable (0%), real output grows by 3%. Inflation ≈ 8% + 0% − 3% = 5%.

  • IdeaExpectations and the wage-price spiral

    If workers expect 6% inflation, they ask for 6% raises. Firms pay them and raise prices to cover the cost, so the expectation comes true. This loop is the wage-price spiral. It is why central banks care about what people expect, not only about today's number.

4

Who wins, who loses

Inflation does not hurt everyone equally. What matters is whether it was expected.

  • WzórNominal vs real interest rate (Fisher)

    real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation exact: (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1

    • PrzykładA savings account

      The bank pays 6% a year, inflation is 4%. Real return ≈ 6% − 4% = 2% (exactly 1.06 / 1.04 − 1 ≈ 1.92%). If inflation were 7%, the real return would be negative: your savings would buy less, even though the balance grows.

  • IdeaUnexpected inflation moves wealth

    • Debtors gain: they repay a fixed loan with money that is worth less.
    • Creditors and savers lose if the interest rate did not include the inflation that came.
    • People on fixed incomes lose (a fixed salary or rent that is not indexed).

    When inflation is expected, lenders build it into the interest rate, so the transfer is much smaller.

  • IdeaCosts of inflation, even when expected

    • Shoe-leather costs: time and effort spent holding less cash and moving money around.
    • Menu costs: the cost of changing prices (new price lists, systems, menus).
    • Uncertainty: firms delay investment when they cannot predict prices.
    • Distorted signals: it is harder to tell whether a product is really getting dearer or all prices are rising.

  • Typowy błąd"Deflation is good, things get cheaper"

    When prices are expected to fall, people postpone purchases, firms sell less and cut jobs, and the real value of debts grows. That is why central banks aim for low positive inflation, not zero or negative.

5

How it is fought

The main tool is the central bank's interest rate. Fiscal policy (taxes, spending) also matters.

  • DefinicjaInflation target

    The NBP's Monetary Policy Council (RPP) aims for CPI inflation of 2.5% with a tolerance band of ±1 percentage point. A known target anchors expectations (see 3, expectations). Sprawdź aktualne: the current rate and inflation figures change every month (nbp.pl, stat.gov.pl).

  • IdeaThe interest-rate chain

    1. The central bank raises its reference rate.
    2. Loans get dearer, saving pays more.
    3. Households and firms borrow and spend less: aggregate demand falls.
    4. With weaker demand, firms raise prices more slowly: inflation falls, usually after 1–2 years.

    • Nie mylWorks best on demand-pull

      Higher rates cool demand. Against a cost-push shock (energy prices) they also reduce output, so the bank faces a harder choice.

    • Połączenie

      Money, banks and monetary policy: Lekcja 6

Jak to się łączy

  1. 2 → 4: you need the inflation rate from the CPI to compute the real interest rate.
  2. 3 → 5: rate rises work by reducing aggregate demand, so they fit demand-pull inflation best.
  3. 3 (expectations) → 5 (target): a credible target stops the wage-price spiral before it starts.
  4. 1 → Lekcja 3: real GDP is nominal GDP corrected with the deflator from branch 2.
